# Someone Cloned My Father's Instagram. Then They Did It Again.

And again. Here's everything we learned, and what you need to know.

My father is not a public figure. He's a regular person with a modest Instagram following, mostly family, old colleagues, and friends from his community. So when the first fake account appeared, we thought it was a one-off. We reported it. We blocked it. Done.

Except it wasn't done.

A new clone showed up within days. Same name with a double underscore. Different photos, taken from God knows where. And this time, some of his friends had already received a message:

> *"Hey, I'm in a difficult situation. Can you send me some money? I'll explain later."*

By the time we caught the third account, we stopped treating this as a nuisance and started treating it as a serious problem. This blog is everything we've learned — not just from our experience, but from researching how this actually works and what you can realistically do about it.

If this is happening to you, your parent, or someone close to you, keep reading.

---

## Why This Keeps Happening (And Why Reporting Alone Doesn't Stop It)

This was the most frustrating part to accept: reporting and blocking are necessary, but not sufficient.

Here's why:

Creating a fake Instagram account takes roughly 4 minutes. A throwaway email address, a VPN, and a few publicly visible photos are all an attacker needs. When a fake account gets removed, the same person can spin up a new one almost immediately, with a slightly tweaked username and a fresh set of photos.

Platforms like Instagram and Facebook remove billions of fake accounts every year. But they're fighting at scale. Targeted impersonation of one specific individual — especially someone who isn't a celebrity or brand — often slips through the automated detection nets repeatedly.

The attacker also has a strong motivation to keep going: money. A single successful DM asking for emergency funds, gift cards, or a money transfer can net a scammer hundreds of dollars. It costs them almost nothing to try again with the next clone.

And here's the uncomfortable truth: **the trust your loved one has built over a lifetime is the weapon being used against their friends.** The scam works not because it's technically sophisticated, but because people trust someone they recognize.

---

## How Profile Cloning Actually Works

Understanding the mechanics helps you see what to fix. Here's the typical playbook:

**Step 1 — They collect your public data.**
Name, profile photo, bio, and public posts are all scraped. Follower lists are especially valuable — that's the target list. Every person who publicly follows your father is a potential victim.

**Step 2 — They build a convincing look-alike.**
Same display name. Similar username with a small variation: an underscore, a number, or a period. A few reposted public photos to make the profile look "real." Sometimes the fake account is set to public to appear open and trustworthy.

**Step 3 — They reach out to the victim's network.**
The opening message is usually low-stakes: *"Hey, this is my new account, my old one got hacked."* Once people accept the follow or reply, the real ask comes next: an emergency, a stranded situation, an investment tip, a "code" they urgently need you to share.

**Step 4 — When blocked, they rebuild.**
This is the loop we were stuck in. One report. One removal. One new clone. Repeat.

## Where Do the "Private" Photos Come From?

This question haunted us the most. Some photos appearing on the fake accounts had never been publicly posted. So where did they come from?

A few likely sources:

- **A compromised account:** If the real Instagram account were ever hacked, even briefly, the attacker would have had access to archived posts, tagged photos, and photos shared in private DMs.
- **A hacked cloud backup:** Photos on iCloud, Google Photos, or similar services can expose your entire camera roll if the linked email or cloud credentials were ever breached.
- **Group chats and private messages:** Photos shared in WhatsApp groups, Messenger chats, or Discord servers can be saved and forwarded without the original sender knowing.
- **Someone in the social circle:** This is uncomfortable to consider, but sometimes a disgruntled acquaintance, ex-partner, or even a mutual contact can supply or leak personal photos.
- **AI-generated variations:** Increasingly, scammers use AI tools to generate slightly different versions of a person's face, so the profile appears to have a variety of original photos even when the source material is limited.

If you're seeing photos of someone that appear nowhere online, it usually means the source wasn't public — which points to either a past compromise or a human leak.

## What To Do Right Now (If This Is Happening to You)

Here is a practical, step-by-step response plan. No jargon. Just what to actually do.

### 1. Document before you report

Before tapping "Report" and the profile disappears, take screenshots of:
- The fake profile and username
- Any posts or stories on it
- Messages the impersonator has sent to friends

This is your evidence. You'll need it if things escalate.

### 2. Warn the network publicly and immediately

Post a clear statement from the real account. Something like:

> *"There is a fake account impersonating me. Please do not accept follow requests or respond to DMs from any profile that is not this one. Never send money or personal information based on a social media message — always call me directly to verify."*

This single step can stop significant financial damage. Attackers count on people not knowing that a scam is in progress.

### 3. Report the impersonation using the right method

- **In-app reporting on Instagram:** Go to the fake profile → tap the three dots → Report → "It's pretending to be someone else" → "Me" or "Someone I know"
- **Use the formal impersonation form:** Instagram's dedicated "Report an Impersonation Account" form allows you to submit a government-issued ID to prove your identity. This carries more weight than in-app reporting.
- **Ask your network to report it too.** Multiple reports from different accounts signal an urgent need for the platform's moderation systems.

### 4. Secure the real account immediately

Even if you believe the real account hasn't been hacked, treat it as potentially compromised:
- Change the password to something long, unique, and used nowhere else.
- Enable two-factor authentication — use an authenticator app (like Google Authenticator or Authy), not just SMS.
- Go to the login activity and revoke any sessions you don't recognize.
- Remove any third-party apps connected to the account that you don't use or recognize.

### 5. Check connected accounts and devices

Social media compromise usually starts somewhere else — often via email. Make sure:
- Your email account (especially the one tied to Instagram) has a unique password and 2FA enabled.
- Cloud storage accounts (iCloud, Google Photos) are secured the same way.
- Run a malware or antivirus scan on the primary device, especially if it's a phone.

### 6. Escalate legally if the situation is serious

If the impersonation involves financial fraud, defamation, harassment, sexual content, or extortion, don't just rely on platform reporting. Report to:
- Your local police, with your screenshots as evidence
- The **Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre** (1-888-495-8501 or [antifraudcentre.ca](http://antifraudcentre.ca)) if you're in Canada
- In India, call **1930** first — this is the single most important number. After submitting your complaint, you'll receive a 14-digit acknowledgment number to track the case. If money is lost, this system also alerts banks to freeze and trace the funds.
- The **Canadian Centre for Cyber Security** ([cyber.gc.ca](http://cyber.gc.ca)), or if in India, [cybercrime.gov.in](http://cybercrime.gov.in) for guidance on escalation.

> **Impersonation tied to financial fraud is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions. Document everything.**

## If It Escalates: Know the Law and Who to Call

### 🇮🇳 India — Applicable Laws

- **IT Act, Section 66C** — Identity theft using a fake social media profile
- **IT Act, Section 66D** — Cheating by impersonation using online means
- **IPC Section 420** — Financial fraud and cheating
- **IPC Section 499 / 500** — Defamation or reputational harm caused by the fake account

These are criminal offences. You can reference them when filing a complaint with the police or the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal.

## Long-Term Precautions: Making Yourself a Harder Target

You can't make it impossible for someone to clone your profile. But you can make it significantly harder and less rewarding for them.

### Lock down your privacy settings

- **Set your account to private.** This is the single highest-impact change, especially for family members who don't need a public profile.
- **Hide your followers/following list.** On Facebook, set this to "Only Me." On Instagram, a private account limits who can see your connections.
- **Approve tags manually.** Go to Settings → Tags → Manually Approve Tags. This prevents others from tagging you in photos you haven't reviewed.

### Be selective about who follows you

Every person who follows a public account is a window into the victim's network. For older family members, apply a simple filter: if you don't know them in person, don't accept.

### Use strong, unique passwords everywhere

Reused passwords are how most accounts get compromised. Use a password manager (like Bitwarden or 1Password) to generate and store unique passwords for every account.

### Set a "verification rule" with your close circle

Have a direct conversation with family and close friends. Say something like:

> *"If you ever get a message from me on social media asking for money, a code, or something urgent, call me on my phone before you do anything. I will never ask for money through DMs."*

This one habit would prevent the vast majority of these scams from succeeding. The scam relies on fast action and emotional pressure. A simple "let me call you first" breaks both.

### Periodically search for fake profiles

Every few months, search your name (or a family member's name) on Instagram, Facebook, and Google Images to see if clones have been created. Catching them early limits the damage.

## A Note on the Emotional Toll

The technical steps matter. But I want to acknowledge something that doesn't get said enough:

**The experience of being impersonated is violating.**

My father felt embarrassed that friends might think he was begging for money. He felt anxious every time a friend called to ask if he was okay. He questioned who in his circle might have seen the fake account and believed it.

That emotional weight is real, and it sits alongside all the practical steps you have to take. If you're managing this on behalf of a parent or older relative, check in on how they're feeling — not just on the status of their account settings.

---

## Three Things to Do Today

You've made it to the end. Here's your immediate action list:

1. **Review the privacy settings** on your own Instagram and Facebook right now — especially whether your followers list is visible and whether your account is public.
2. **Have a 5-minute conversation** with one parent, sibling, or older relative about what to do if they ever receive a suspicious money request from "you" online.
3. **Enable 2FA on your email account** — not just your social accounts. Email is often the real entry point for account compromises.

The more we talk openly about how these scams work, the harder they become to pull off. Share this with someone who needs it.
