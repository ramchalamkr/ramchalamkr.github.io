# Why Smart, Security-Aware People Still Fall for Phishing?

*Published on LinkedIn Pulse · March 2026 · [View original on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/why-smart-security-aware-people-still-fall-phishing-rajamohan-m7kje/)*

*Tags: #Cyberpsychology #Phishing #Cybersecurity #HumanFactors #Infosec*

---

I still remember the first time I watched a "very smart" colleague click a malicious link.

They weren't careless. They weren't untrained. They were tired, rushing to finish work, juggling chats and emails, and trying to be helpful. That's when it really hit me: phishing is not about intelligence. It's about being human.

In this article, I want to look at phishing not as a technical problem, but as a human one. When we bring a bit of cyberpsychology into the picture, our inboxes make a lot more sense.

---

## It's not a "user problem" — it's a human brain problem

Cyberpsychology is just a fancy word for "how people think, feel, and behave when technology is involved."

When we apply that lens to phishing, some patterns jump out:

- People want to be helpful.
- People don't want to upset their boss.
- People are busy, distracted, and exhausted.
- People act from emotion first, logic second.

Attackers know this very well. Most phishing campaigns are less about technical brilliance and more about understanding our fears, habits, and hopes.

---

## Four very human reasons people click

### 1. We respect authority (and we don't want to say no)

If an email looks like it's from a CEO, manager, or someone "important," a tiny voice in our head says:

> *"Don't make them wait."*  
> *"Just get it done."*

So when we see:

> *"Hi, can you urgently process this payment?"*

Many people act first, question later. It's not about not knowing what phishing is. It's about not wanting to risk upsetting someone senior.

---

### 2. Urgency hijacks our calm brain

How many phishing emails sound like this?

- *"Your account will be locked in 24 hours."*
- *"Immediate action required to avoid penalties."*

The second we feel that jolt of anxiety, our brain switches into "fix it now" mode. We read faster. We think less. We click more. It's the same reason we rush through tasks right before a deadline. The email becomes "just one more thing to clear" so we can breathe again.

---

### 3. Curiosity and reward feel good

Not all phishing is scary. Some of it is tempting:

- *"Updated bonus scheme attached."*
- *"Exclusive invite just for you."*
- *"You've been selected…"*

Some of us know this could be suspicious. Another part of us thinks:

> *"What if this is real? I don't want to miss it."*

That tiny "what if" is often enough to push a click, especially on a long day when you've had nothing but problems and finally see something that looks like good news.

---

### 4. Our habits run on autopilot

Most of us process emails like this:

> Open → skim → click link / open attachment → move on.

We don't treat each email like a crime scene to be investigated. If we did, we'd never finish our work. So when an email looks close enough to "yet another normal request," our brain doesn't spin up full detective mode. It runs on autopilot. That's exactly the state attackers are hoping for.

This is why even experienced, security-aware people can be caught off guard at 4:45 PM on a Friday.

---

## A simple story from a normal workday

Picture this:

You're in the middle of a busy shift. Tickets are piling up. A chat window keeps blinking. Your calendar says you're already behind.

Then an email drops in:

> **From:** "Finance Director"  
> **Subject:** "Urgent: payment before 5 PM."  
>  
> *"We need this processed today. Penalties if delayed. Use this new portal link; the old one is down. Thanks for acting quickly on this."*

In that moment, what feels riskier?

- Slowing down, questioning the email, maybe annoying someone senior?
- Or just quickly doing it so you can move on?

If you're honest, you know the answer. And that's the point.

**Phishing isn't picking on "the weakest link." It's picking on the most human moments.**

---

## What we can do differently as security people

If we only respond with more training, more simulated phishing, and more "don't click" emails — we're missing the core issue: people are doing their best in messy, stressful environments.

Here are a few shifts that make everything more humane **and** more effective:

### 1. Design for real behavior, not for "perfect users."

- Reduce how often people have to click links to do important tasks.
- Use known portals and predictable processes.
- Make unusual requests stand out in a very obvious way.

If the safe path is easy and familiar, people are more likely to take it.

---

### 2. Talk about emotions, not just "red flags."

Instead of only teaching *"look at the sender, check the URL,"* we should also say:

- *"If you feel rushed, scared, or pressured, that's your sign to slow down."*
- *"If an email makes you feel panic or excitement, pause before you act."*

Teaching people to notice their own emotional state is just as important as teaching them to spot technical clues.

---

### 3. Make "I need to verify this" a strength, not a risk

A lot of people know something feels off, but they don't feel safe saying:

> *"Let me double-check this."*

Leaders can help by:

- Publicly praising people who verified something suspicious.
- Sharing real stories of *"We almost lost money, but someone spoke up."*
- Making it clear that no one gets in trouble for double-checking.

When people feel psychologically safe, they're far more likely to trust their instincts.

---

## A tiny checklist for your next suspicious email

Next time an email demands something from you, try this:

- **Ask yourself:** *"How am I feeling right now?"* Tired, rushed, anxious, or excited? → **Slow down.**
- **Ask:** *"Would this person normally ask me this in this way?"*
- **For money, passwords, and sensitive data:** verify in another channel (a known phone number or internal chat).

These are small steps, but they protect real people and real organizations.

---

## Final thought

When we treat phishing as a purely technical issue, we end up blaming users. When we treat it as a human issue, we start redesigning systems, processes, and culture to support the way people actually think and feel at work.

That's the shift I'd love to see more of:

**Less blame, more understanding, and security that is built *with* humans in mind — not against them.**
